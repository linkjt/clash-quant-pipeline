# clash-quant-pipeline

## The Premise
Clash Royale is a competitive video game where players go head-to-head placing cards strategically in order to try to destroy the opponents princess and king towers. In Clash Royale, players can choose from 171 different cards to build an 8 card deck. There are over 7.4 trillion possible deck combinations. It is computationally impossible to store data about every single deck. Sites like RoyaleAPI and StatRoyale attempt to track deck winrates, however slight modifications for decks could drastically impact winrates. Additionally, even if individual decks are tracked, there is no guarantee there will be enough games for a deck on a specific day where there are modifications made. Additionally, it is impossible to get the win rate of both decks and cards for today, as the only way we can accurately gauge the full data of games is if we are looking backwards. (i.e. every site has win rates for the past day, the past three days, etc). None of them have win rates for today. The answer is to overthink it.
### Stocks
In the stock market we use quant math to predict stock prices using a variety of technical indicators. These indicators alone can predict things; however, utilizing machine learning to even better predict stocks and stuff. This all works because the stock market is competitive, and for a price on a given day, the price the next day is based on the action of whether people buy or sell. This translates to the Clash Royale win-rate. Additionally, if a market doesn't have a lot of money in it, a single buy moves the market a lot more than in a market with a lot of money. This is liquidity. Its Clash Royale equivalent is the usage rate. My idea of this analysis is that Clash Royale analysis is like stock market analysis. It started with quantitative analysis (e.g.companies' new products); but then it moved into quantitative analysis, and that is now the standard. Clash has untapped potential for mathematical prediction. This is the future of Clash Royale analysis. The best deck now. The best deck in 10 days. The best synergy, the worst synergies, when buffs happen, how bad nerfs hit, etc.
## Implementation
### Data Collection
It begins by grabbing raw matches from Clash Royale's API. The method I use is mass collecting every single game in the past 30 days. Although this does skew towards what is shown by any individual deck, the law of numbers says that having more data is overall better. Then I find one random match and look at that person's opponent and then do the same for their games. In this process I save all games to a sql database so I don't save any duplicates. Then I create match ids that correspond to each match so that neither of the matches from the opponent’s game is put in as a new match.
### Data Processing
I process every single match with a script that tallies games played and games won for every card, by day. This is much better than calculating win rates as that would be doing repeated multiplication and this simplifies it down to addition. Here I also differentiate between Evolutions and heros. Additionally I create a table of total cards played for that day to get usage rates for later on.
### Data Analysis
This is what's novel. I start out by creating a bunch of indicators: RSI, ROC, Sharpe Ratio, Beta, Drawdown, and more. This is really simple stuff that I just use for creating the preliminary first step to the more complicated math. I also created a correlation matrix of all cards based purely on winrate to mathematically find synergies. This is used for what is called in quant pair divergence and pair convergences. Basically two correlated cards should have winrates that stick together, and drifting between them will eventually result in reversion. These correlations are then used to create 6 different card clusters of correlated cards which are huge in predicting trends in winrates. Additionally, I have two predictive models that are both really simple, Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) Process. These models sound complicated but they just are Monte Carlo simulations where they only have the past volatility and use that. The only difference is the GBM is not meant reverting and the OU is mean reverting. They are often wrong and their predictions are useless for short term and since its mean reverting is really just random long term. As a result, I use a bit more complicated math for the next step, Machine Learning. I have two models that I use, XGBoost and LSTM. XGBoost is a classification model and I use the LSTM for regression. The XGBoost is trained on all cards all at once and predicts whether or not the cards' win rate will be higher tomorrow utilizing all of the different quant metrics that I have created. It is accurate over 70% of the time. In quant that would be insane. However it is useless if a card's winrate is high if we can't figure out the actual winrate, which is what the LSTM does. It uses a very similar feature table but it is trained on a 7 day window. This method predicts based on data up until yesterday, so it predicts for today, effectively giving us today's win rate. This solves the first major problem with other analytic sites. Say you are a new player, you want to spend your gold on good long term cards, those cards can be mathematically found.
## Application
### Decks
Finally after one month, deck generation is real. Originally my plan was to use the sectors I generated based on the large matrix card correlations based purely upon win rate movement. However this neglects that card movement relates to similar cards’ functionality. I determined I needed to find a way to create relationships between cards based on their functionality so that I wasn't swapping hog rider for skeletons, for example. First, I thought of trying to list every possible different card and grouping their functionalities; however, this would take too long so I kept thinking. Finally I decided on doing it by natural language processing. I gave in to storing decks, but only storing the decks themselves and the number of times they have been played in whole. This severely cut down on the size of the database. Then I do natural language processing with it to look at the arrangement of decks to see which decks look similar based on which cards. This gives me functional relationships of each card. I used Gensim for this and it really helped and is great at swapping similar cards. Now decks can be generated better than ever and swaps can be made that make sense and boost win rates of cards. The only issue is just balancing out weights, as sometimes it weighs XGBoost probabilities higher than relations. Other than that, deck generation is real.
